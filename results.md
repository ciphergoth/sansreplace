# Results

These results were measured on an Apple M4 MacBook Pro with 16GB of memory, using clang 21.1 and
rustc 1.98, for values of _n_ and _k_ that are Fibonacci numbers, with _k_ at most 102,334,155
(the 40th Fibonacci number). [Full results](results/latest/)

Each algorithm is timed in its own run, and its CSVs record the commit and setup it was timed with.

Since both _n_ and _k_ affect the performance, we compare algorithms
using an animated graph. For each frame, we fix the value of _n_ and
graph the performance of each algorithm for various values of _k_;
we graph time/_k_ to show the cost in ns per number we output. Since
we are plotting a linear/log graph of time/_k_, the _k_ log _k_
performance of `cardchoose` is visible as a straight line.

## Choosing algorithms to cover every case

No single algorithm is close to the fastest everywhere, so a good implementation will implement
several and choose between them based on _n_ and _k_. [Choosing algorithms](choosing.md) gives the
best sets of one, two or three algorithms in each case, by two different measures, and the
trade-off between them.

By this measure three algorithms are never far from the fastest: `cardchoose`, `hiddenshuffle` and
`vitterd` are never more than 27 ns per number slower in any case, while every other algorithm is at
least 97 ns slower at some point where it was timed, and all but `hsel` over 100 ns. `cardchoose` is
the simplest of the three: it is a few lines of integer arithmetic, while the other two need
floating point and are considerably more involved. `vitterd` switches to Algorithm A once _n_ ≤ 13
_k_, and `hiddenshuffle` runs three stages in turn, the last of them Algorithm A. `cardchoose`'s
worst point in every case is at the largest _k_, with _n_ the next Fibonacci number or so above it,
where the O(_n_) algorithms are fastest; at _k_ = _n_ itself it has almost nothing to do. Along
those points its excess grows slowly with _k_, as the log _k_ factor of its sort would suggest.

For every pair of algorithms, the pairwise comparisons give how much slower one is than the other

## C++

The C++ implementations use only the standard library: `std::unordered_set` and
`std::unordered_map`, which are node-based, and `std::sort`. So these results show the
performance you get from standard components.

For sorted output, `hiddenshuffle` is the fastest algorithm wherever _n_ ≥ 256 _k_, and wherever _n_
≥ 16 _k_ and _k_ ≥ 1000. There, `cardchoose` takes up to 2.5× as long, and `ranksb` 1.09–1.9× as
long. `ranksb` slows down as _k_ approaches _n_, where Nijenhuis and Wilf pair it with
`iterativechoose` (which they refer to as RKS2). For random output with _k_ ≥ 1000, `select` is the
fastest where 2 _k_ ≤ _n_ < 16 _k_, and `hiddenshuffle` followed by a shuffle is the fastest where
_n_ ≥ 256 _k_. `cardchoose` is faster than `rejectionsample`, `floydf2` and `hsel` at every point
where _k_ ≤ _n_/2: they take 1.01–4.0× as long, and at least 1.27× as long where _n_ ≥ 4 _k_.

## Rust

The Rust implementations use Rust's standard `HashMap`, `HashSet` and unstable sort. The hash
tables are flat open-addressing tables, much faster than the node-based C++ ones; the hash-based
algorithms take about 0.4–0.5× as long in Rust as in C++, and `cardchoose` about 0.6–0.7×, while
`ranksb`, `vitterd` and `hiddenshuffle` take about the same time in both.

For sorted output, `cardchoose` and `hiddenshuffle` divide the range by _k_. Wherever _k_ < 1000 and
_n_ ≥ 2 _k_, `cardchoose` is the fastest algorithm or within 25% of it, and `hiddenshuffle` takes up
to 4.0× as long as `cardchoose`. Wherever _k_ ≥ 10<sup>5</sup> and _n_ ≥ 16 _k_, `hiddenshuffle` is
the fastest algorithm or within 3% of it, and `cardchoose` takes up to 1.6× as long. In between,
they trade places. For random output, `select` is the fastest wherever _n_ < 16 _k_, and `hsel` is
within 15% of the fastest wherever _n_ ≥ 256 _k_ and _k_ ≥ 1000. `cardchoose` is never more than
1.50× slower than the fastest of `rejectionsample`, `floydf2`, `hsel` and itself where _k_ ≤ _n_/2.
