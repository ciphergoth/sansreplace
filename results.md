# Results

These results were measured on an Apple M4 MacBook Pro with 16GB of memory, using clang 21.1 and
rustc 1.98, for values of _n_ and _k_ that are Fibonacci numbers, with _k_ at most 102,334,155
(the 40th Fibonacci number). [Full results](results/latest/)

Each algorithm is timed in its own run, and its CSVs record the commit and setup it was timed
with. Hidden Shuffle was timed a few hours after the others, on the same setup.

Since both _n_ and _k_ affect the performance, we compare algorithms
using an animated graph. For each frame, we fix the value of _n_ and
graph the performance of each algorithm for various values of _k_;
we graph time/_k_ to show the cost in ns per number we output. Since
we are plotting a linear/log graph of time/_k_, the _k_ log _k_
performance of `cardchoose` is visible as a straight line.

## Choosing algorithms to cover every case

No single algorithm is close to the fastest everywhere, so a good implementation will implement
several and choose between them based on _n_ and _k_. For each language and order, the tables give
the set of one, two or three algorithms that comes closest to the fastest of all of them at every
point with _k_ ≥ 10, if each point uses the fastest algorithm in the set. This assumes the best
choice is made at every point; an implementation has to choose by a rule, such as a threshold on
_n_/_k_. Vitter's Algorithm D is itself two algorithms, since it switches to Algorithm A once
_n_ ≤ 13 _k_, so wherever the best set includes it, the tables also give the best set without it.

There are two ways to measure how close a set comes to the fastest, and each has a flaw:

* **Ratio**: the largest factor by which the set is slower than the fastest. Its worst case is
  often at a tiny _k_, where the stakes are tiny too. The best two algorithms for C++ random
  output by this measure are 3.01× slower than the fastest at _n_ = 4181, _k_ = 13, but that is
  98 ns against 33 ns, only 65 ns more per call.
* **Difference**: the most the set's time/_k_ exceeds the fastest's, which is the cost per number
  output. This discounts tiny _k_, but ignores large relative losses where everything is cheap.
  The best three algorithms for Rust sorted output by this measure are only 2.12 ns per number
  slower than the fastest at worst, but at _n_ = _k_ = 10<sup>8</sup> that makes them 7.93×
  slower: 0.25 s against 0.031 s.

What a caller probably wants is somewhere in between: being a few times slower doesn't matter on
a call that takes nanoseconds, but does on one that takes a quarter of a second. So we give the
best sets by each measure, each with its worst ratio and worst difference, which needn't occur at
the same point. Generated by [`python/cover`](python/cover) `--measure ratio` and
`--measure difference`.

By this measure three algorithms are never far from the fastest: `cardchoose`, `hiddenshuffle`
and `vitterd` are never more than 26 ns per number slower in any case, while every other algorithm
is at least 35 ns slower at some point where it was timed, and most over 100 ns. `cardchoose` is
the simplest of the three: it is a few lines of integer arithmetic, while the other two need
floating point and are considerably more involved. `vitterd` switches to Algorithm A once
_n_ ≤ 13 _k_, and `hiddenshuffle` runs three stages in turn, the last of them Algorithm A.
`cardchoose`'s worst point in every case is at the largest _k_, with _n_ the next Fibonacci number
or so above it, where the O(_n_) algorithms are fastest; at _k_ = _n_ itself it has almost nothing
to do. Along those points its excess grows slowly with _k_, as the log _k_ factor of its sort would
suggest.

one algorithm's time over another's, or over the fastest of all the others, in bands of _n_/_k_
and _k_.

## Each pair of algorithms

For every pair of algorithms, the pairwise comparisons give how much slower one is than the other

## C++

The C++ implementations use only the standard library: `std::unordered_set` and
`std::unordered_map`, which are node-based, and `std::sort`. So these results show the
performance you get from standard components.

For sorted output, `hiddenshuffle` is the fastest algorithm wherever _n_ ≥ 256 _k_, and wherever
_n_ ≥ 16 _k_ and _k_ ≥ 1000. There, `cardchoose` takes up to 2.5× as long, and `ranksb` 1.09–1.9×
as long. `ranksb` slows down as _k_ approaches _n_, where Nijenhuis and Wilf pair it with
`iterativechoose` (which they refer to as RKS2). For random output with _k_ ≥ 1000, `select` is
the fastest, or within 10% of it, where 2 _k_ ≤ _n_ < 16 _k_, and `hiddenshuffle` followed by a
shuffle is the fastest where _n_ ≥ 256 _k_. `cardchoose` is faster than `rejectionsample`,
`floydf2` and `hsel` at every point where _k_ ≤ _n_/2: they take 1.02–4.7× as long, and at least
1.28× as long where _n_ ≥ 4 _k_.

## Rust

The Rust implementations use Rust's standard `HashMap`, `HashSet` and unstable sort. The hash
tables are flat open-addressing tables, much faster than the node-based C++ ones; the hash-based
algorithms take about 0.4–0.5× as long in Rust as in C++, and `cardchoose` about 0.6–0.7×, while
`ranksb`, `vitterd` and `hiddenshuffle` take about the same time in both.

For sorted output, `cardchoose` and `hiddenshuffle` divide the range by _k_. Wherever _k_ < 1000
and _n_ ≥ 2 _k_, `cardchoose` is the fastest algorithm or within 25% of it, and `hiddenshuffle`
takes up to 3.9× as long as `cardchoose`. Wherever _k_ ≥ 10<sup>5</sup> and _n_ ≥ 16 _k_,
`hiddenshuffle` is the fastest algorithm or within 3% of it, and `cardchoose` takes up to 1.5× as
long. In between, they trade places. For random output, `select` is the fastest, or within 4% of
it, wherever _n_ < 16 _k_, and `hsel` is within 15% of the fastest wherever _n_ ≥ 256 _k_ and
_k_ ≥ 1000. `cardchoose` is never more than 1.51× slower than the fastest of `rejectionsample`,
`floydf2`, `hsel` and itself where _k_ ≤ _n_/2.
